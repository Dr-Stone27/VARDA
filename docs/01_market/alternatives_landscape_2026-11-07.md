# Alternatives landscape: what customers can already use

**Research date:** 7 November 2026  
**Stage:** Phase 1, Stage 1C — Alternatives analysis  
**Status:** Draft for founder review; Stage 1D has not started

## Decision question

What categories of substitutes can address the needs implied by H1 (reliability/assurance), H2 (integration/orchestration), H3 (context/knowledge), and pre-adoption guidance? What can public evidence establish about their capabilities and limitations, and what remains unknown about customer sufficiency?

## Executive readout

The incumbent landscape is broad and overlapping. Cloud platforms document native agent building, evaluation, tracing, monitoring, identity, governance and operational controls. Workflow/iPaaS tools connect applications and provide automation or agent actions. Specialist AI development tools offer datasets, evaluators, trace review and deployment-time monitoring. Enterprise search/context vendors connect company systems, index knowledge and enforce permissions. In some vertical ecosystems, such as Shopify, an embedded assistant can create workflows using native automation capabilities. Large consultancies and cloud partners publicly offer strategy, integration, governance, implementation and managed services.

This is enough to reject the claim that customers have no available alternatives. It is **not** enough to conclude that alternatives are adequate for a given workflow or customer. The capability claims are mostly vendor or provider documentation; we found no independent comparative evaluation establishing effectiveness, no customer evidence showing repeated gaps, and no willingness-to-pay evidence for an independent Varda offer.

Some product docs state meaningful boundaries: connectors vary in freshness/access modes; workflow steps depend on available connectors and input data; one agent product is being migrated to a successor and has restrictions on sharing/customer-facing use; LLM evaluators require calibration and human review. These are product-specific constraints and research leads, not proof of an unmet market gap.

## Where evidence of limitations exists

1. **Instrumentation and evaluation are not automatic guarantees.** Platform observability depends on traces/telemetry being available; LangSmith explicitly says LLM-as-judge can be wrong and recommends human review and calibration. This creates a need for sound evaluation design but does not prove a separate vendor is needed.
2. **Integration coverage has boundaries.** Glean documents connector-specific retrieval/freshness behavior and setup dependencies. Shopify Flow says not all apps have connectors, and correct data must be passed into each action. Product capability is therefore conditional on systems, plans, configuration and data shape.
3. **Products have different deployment and governance envelopes.** Zapier’s documented Agents experience has sharing/customer-facing and usage constraints and is transitioning to AI by Zapier. Workato and major clouds offer more centralized enterprise controls, but market fit/cost for smaller firms is not established here.
4. **Service-provider alternatives are difficult to compare from public material.** Offers are broad and overlapping, but public descriptions rarely establish project scope, total cost, client-side effort, realized outcomes or suitability for smaller businesses.
5. **No independent evidence found in this stage establishes the prevalence or cost of failures caused by any of these limitations.** Product limitations should be validated against customer workflows before calling them a market gap.

## Implications by hypothesis (no ranking)

| Hypothesis | What the alternatives map changes | Unresolved test |
|---|---|---|
| H1 — Reliability and assurance | Strong feature overlap exists among cloud-native evaluation/observability and specialist tools. A separate offer must address a demonstrable gap in evaluation design, implementation, cross-platform operations, governance or business accountability; feature existence alone weakens broad “no tools” framing. | In concrete workflows, what failures go unseen or unmanaged despite the tools/processes already available? What do internal owners actually use, and where does it fail? |
| H2 — Integration and orchestration | Broad iPaaS, APIs, agents, MCP gateways, native connectors and vertical automation are already available. “AI cannot connect to business systems” is too broad without naming systems, workflow and connector/permission/maintenance constraint. | Walk through a failed or stalled workflow; identify whether the binding constraint was technical connector coverage, data quality, access, process design, capacity, change management or ROI. |
| H3 — Context and knowledge | Enterprise search/context products explicitly offer permissions-aware connectors, knowledge graphs, retrieval and agent actions; this materially challenges a generic context-document/product thesis. Coverage, freshness, sources and relevance are conditional. | For a real wrong/incomplete output, test whether the root cause was missing context, stale/incorrect source material, retrieval, permissions, process, model reasoning or user input—and what current knowledge products were already in place. |
| H0 — Incumbent sufficiency | The landscape includes cloud platforms, AI application vendors, workflow platforms, vertical-native features, internal build paths and providers. This makes H0 credible and requires checking actual use, not merely product catalogs. | Which existing arrangement solves the job adequately, what is still manual, and what is the consequence of leaving it as-is? Evidence must come from customers and observed workflows, not vendor comparison alone. |
| Pre-adoption guidance | Cloud and consulting providers advertise strategy, use-case identification and implementation help; embedded assistants lower the effort to experiment. These are real substitutes for a standalone advisory concept. | Does a specific buyer actively seek guidance, what decision has stalled, what alternatives were considered, and what evidence shows urgency or budget? |

## What remains unsupported or unknown

- No independent head-to-head product test was conducted. Feature tables should not be read as verified performance claims.
- No customer, procurement, implementation-partner or internal-team interview has been conducted for this stage. No customer-reported evidence was added.
- Product docs do not show actual usage, configuration quality, adoption, customer satisfaction, failure rates or realized customer outcomes.
- No price comparison is attempted: public prices are tiered, usage-based, quote-based and subject to change, and service engagements are often not priced publicly. Current cost to solve a specific workflow remains UNKNOWN.
- Enterprise examples and vendor offerings do not establish fit for smaller firms or Varda’s previously assumed DTC segment.
- “Integration”, “reliability” and “context” overlap in real workflows. A tool limitation cannot identify a primary customer problem without a workflow-level account.
- Availability, regional eligibility, plan restrictions and feature names can change; records reflect sources accessed 7 Nov 2026 and should be rechecked before purchase or customer advice.

## Stage 1C completion status

**Condition assessment:** The landscape now covers platform-native capabilities, specialist software, workflow/iPaaS, enterprise context/search, vertical-native capabilities, internal build/status quo, consultancies and managed services. Provider-stated limitations are separated from independent evidence; the absence of independent customer-sufficiency evidence is explicit. Recommend Stage 1C for founder review.

**Recommended next stage:** Stage 1D — hypothesis testing, after founder approval. No hypothesis or segment is selected here. Stage 1D should test observable distinctions and seek counter-evidence, not simply expand this vendor list.
