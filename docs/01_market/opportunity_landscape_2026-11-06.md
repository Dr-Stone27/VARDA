# Opportunity landscape: business AI adoption and operational use

**Research date:** 6 November 2026  
**Stage:** Phase 1, Stage 1B — Market landscape  
**Status:** Draft for founder review; Stage 1C has not started

## Decision question

What do current primary sources establish about business AI adoption and operational use, what problems do they report, and what remains unproven about demand for Varda?

## Executive readout

Official surveys from the UK, EU and US show AI use spreading, but the rates are not directly comparable: definitions, populations, survey designs and reference periods differ. Across sources, reported activity is concentrated in text, administration, marketing/sales and IT, and is often exploratory, augmentative or limited to a few functions. This is evidence of adoption and use, not by itself evidence of an acute, costly customer problem.

UK DSIT reports skills and expertise gaps, concerns about accuracy and bias, and implementation effort. Its same report also describes modest productivity gains, no immediate financial effect for most adopters, modest/no expenditure for many, and low high/critical strategic importance. These are important counter-signals to any broad claim that operational AI problems are universally urgent. A selected study of agent teams documents reliability and evaluation challenges, but does not estimate how common these problems are among ordinary businesses or whether buyers will pay to solve them.

No source reviewed establishes customer willingness to pay, an underserved segment, a standalone market gap, or Varda’s ability to produce better outcomes. Those remain unknown pending customer evidence and, later, alternatives analysis.

## Evidence and interpretation

### 1. Adoption is growing, with important measurement differences

| Finding | Evidence class | Source scope and date | Confidence and limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| 32% of UK businesses reported using at least one AI technology in the 2026 DSIT survey, versus 16% in its 2025 wave. | FACT | DSIT representative UK business survey; 3,503 CATI interviews, 7 Jan–21 Apr 2026; published 29 Oct 2026. [Report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-survey-2026/ai-adoption-survey-report-2026-executive-summary). | High for the published survey estimate. Adoption varies by size and sector; it measures use, not depth, outcome, pain or purchase intent. |
| In September 2026, ONS BICS reported AI use for 30% of all businesses and 38% of businesses with 10+ employees; 51% of businesses with 250+ employees. | FACT | UK private-sector BICS Wave 165, live 21 Sep–4 Oct 2026, published 8 Oct 2026. [ONS bulletin](https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/business/businessservices/bulletins/businessinsightsandimpactontheukeconomy/8october2026). | High for what ONS reports. Voluntary survey; official statistics in development; coverage excludes some sectors, including finance and insurance. The 30% and 38% figures have different population denominators. |
| 20.0% of EU enterprises with at least 10 employees/self-employed persons used AI in 2025, up from 13.5% in 2024. | FACT | Eurostat ICT usage survey, EU, 2025. [Eurostat release](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20251211-2). | High for the published statistic. Survey covers specified NACE sectors and AI technology categories; it cannot be directly compared with UK or US estimates. |
| A US Census Bureau working paper estimates that 18% of firms used AI in a business function in Nov 2025–Jan 2026, rising to 32% when weighted by employment; 22% expected use within six months. | FACT | Nationally representative US BTOS AI supplement; working paper published Apr 2026. [Census paper](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-25.html). | High for paper’s reported results. Working paper, not a customer-demand study; firm-weighted and employment-weighted measures answer different questions. |

**Inference:** Adoption is no longer rare, but headline percentages do not tell us how much use is embedded in consequential workflows. Differences between estimates should be treated as measurement differences unless harmonized definitions and samples support a trend comparison.

### 2. Reported use is concentrated in accessible tasks and a limited number of functions

| Finding | Evidence class | Source scope and date | Confidence and limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| DSIT reports use/planned use most often in administration (81%), marketing (71%) and IT (66%); use is concentrated in accessible, low-complexity tools such as NLP/text generation. | FACT | UK businesses using or planning AI; DSIT 2026 report. | High for report. Denominator is adopters/planners, not all businesses. Categories and responses are self-reported. |
| Eurostat reports written-language analysis (11.8%), image/video/audio generation (9.5%), and written/spoken-language generation (8.8%) as common AI technologies in EU enterprises. | FACT | EU enterprises with 10+ employees, 2025. | High for reported technology categories; use does not indicate production criticality or workflow integration. |
| Among US adopting firms, 57% use AI in three or fewer business functions; leading functions are sales/marketing (52%), strategy/business development (45%) and IT (41%). Writing, document analysis and information search lead worker-task uses. | FACT | US BTOS, Nov 2025–Jan 2026; Census working paper. | High for paper’s observations; adoption and task denominators differ. Paper reports associations, not causal effects. |
| ONS reports text generation with LLMs (21%) and visual content creation (19%) as the most-used technologies among UK businesses with 10+ employees. | FACT | ONS BICS, September 2026. | High for report; voluntary survey and development-statistics caveats apply. |

**Inference:** Current public measures show many assistive and content-oriented use cases, plus some function-level integration. They do not justify assuming widespread autonomous, multi-system workflow operation.

### 3. Sources report barriers, alongside material counter-evidence

| Finding | Evidence class | Source scope and date | Confidence and limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| 67% of UK AI-adopting businesses and 78% of businesses planning adoption reported limited skills/expertise/knowledge; 56% of adopters and 60% of planners reported concerns about bias or accuracy. | FACT | DSIT 2026 UK survey. | High for reported responses. These are perceived barriers among adopters/planners; they do not measure severity, frequency of incidents, loss, budget ownership or willingness to pay. |
| 65% of non-adopters cited lack of a clear business case or proven ROI as a barrier. | FACT | DSIT 2026 UK survey. | High for reported response; could reflect rational restraint, low priority, uncertainty, or unmet enablement needs. It does not establish a paid advisory opportunity. |
| UK adopters reported productivity benefits, but 50% reported an overall productivity increase and the increase was generally modest; 64% reported no immediate financial effect, 24% cost reduction, and 9% cost increase. | FACT | DSIT 2026 UK survey. | High for report; outcomes are self-reported, measures have distinct denominators, and “no immediate financial effect” is not equivalent to no value. |
| Only 14% of UK businesses considered AI tools highly or critically important to operations; 47% of businesses able to estimate expenditure reported no AI expenditure in 2025, reflecting use of free tools or embedded features. | FACT | DSIT 2026 UK survey. | High for report. Low expenditure can signal low spend, bundled value, or early adoption; it neither proves nor disproves a services market. |
| ONS found 38% of UK AI-using businesses with 10+ employees said administrative/clerical roles were most affected; 6% reported reduced headcount due to AI. | FACT | ONS BICS, September 2026. | High for reported responses. “Most affected” is not a positive outcome or a pain measure; official statistics in development and voluntary-response limitations apply. |

**Counter-evidence:** Adoption does not uniformly create costly operational friction. Many businesses report no immediate financial effect, modest gains, no direct AI expenditure, or limited strategic importance. These facts challenge a universal or high-urgency framing. They leave open whether particular workflows, firms or roles experience consequential unresolved problems.

### 4. Production-agent evidence shows technical challenges, not prevalence or buyer demand

Pan et al., *Measuring Agents in Production* (arXiv:2512.04123 v4, 4 Jun 2026; data gathered Apr–Nov 2025) combines 20 case-study interviews and a 306-practitioner survey, with analysis filtered to 86 production/pilot agent systems. It reports reliability among leading development challenges and widespread human involvement in evaluation/operation. Some findings use smaller denominators (for example, N=60 for execution-step claims and N=31 for evaluation methods). [Paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.04123).

- **Evidence class:** FACT about what this study reports.
- **Confidence:** High for the study’s findings; limited generalization because participants and systems are selected, teams are concentrated in the Americas, and the study is not a representative business survey.
- **Counter-evidence / limitation:** The study concerns agent practitioners and deployed/pilot systems, not ordinary business AI use overall; it does not estimate business losses, buyer urgency, or willingness to pay.
- **Implication:** Reliability and oversight are credible technical topics to investigate, but evidence is insufficient to infer a broad customer problem or select H1.

## Implications for the hypotheses (not a ranking)

| Hypothesis | Landscape signal | What remains unknown / next test |
|---|---|---|
| H1 — AI workflow reliability and assurance | Accuracy concerns in UK adopter/planner survey responses and selected agent-system reliability evidence make reliability a plausible investigation topic. | Whether a defined business workflow fails often enough to cause consequential loss; how failures are detected and handled; who owns the problem and current costs. Obtain concrete recent incident narratives from customers and compare against successful/reliable workflows. |
| H2 — AI integration and orchestration | Some sources show use across business functions, and DSIT reports effort to implement/embed AI. Public adoption statistics mostly describe tools/functions, not integration failure. | Whether customers have repeated cross-system integration breakdowns, their frequency and consequence, and whether existing internal or vendor capabilities leave a specific gap. Seek workflow walkthroughs and failed/abandoned implementation examples. |
| H3 — Business context and knowledge infrastructure | Use in writing, search and administration makes context relevant as a possible factor, but none of these sources isolates context quality as the cause of poor outcomes. | Whether missing, stale, fragmented or inaccessible business context causes observable rework/errors; whether context tooling or existing procedures already solve it. Trace actual output failures to context versus other causes. |
| H0 — Existing tools, teams and providers adequately solve the problem | Free and embedded AI use, limited direct spend and assistive usage are consistent with some needs being met adequately or not important enough to address. | Which workflows are adequately served, by whom, and which are not; what triggers external spend or internal intervention. This requires the alternatives analysis and customer evidence; no conclusion is drawn here. |
| Pre-adoption opportunity — deciding where/how AI applies | Lack of business case/ROI and use-case understanding are reported barriers, especially among non-adopters. | Whether this is a high-priority job with a budget, which roles seek help, and whether businesses prefer internal guidance, vendors or services. Evidence of a reported barrier is not evidence of paid demand. |

## Important assumptions still untested

1. Reported skills or accuracy concerns translate into recurring, consequential operational pain rather than general caution.
2. Businesses will pay an external provider to address that pain rather than use embedded tools, internal staff, consultants, or accept the limitation.
3. The problem is concentrated in a reachable customer/workflow combination and is frequent enough to support a repeatable offer.
4. Any reliability, integration or context failure can be attributed to a distinct primary constraint rather than overlapping causes.
5. Adoption rates imply a growing serviceable opportunity; no market size, serviceable market or revenue is established by this scan.

## Evidence gaps and research limitations

- No customer interviews, observed workflow records, incident logs, buyer evidence, spend evidence, or willingness-to-pay evidence are in the canonical record.
- National surveys aggregate firm sizes, sectors and use cases; they cannot identify Varda’s target customer or actionable workflow.
- Most findings above are self-reported. They do not independently verify performance or causal impact.
- UK ONS, UK DSIT, Eurostat and US BTOS differ in definitions, population coverage, survey methods and field dates; cross-source percentages are not a single harmonized time series.
- Technical agent evidence is selected and practitioner-oriented; it cannot be generalized as business-level prevalence.
- Stage 1B has not mapped substitute capabilities. No market gap or incumbent insufficiency is asserted.

## Stage 1B completion status

**Condition assessment:** Market landscape has been documented from current official statistics and a primary technical study, including scope, caveats, counter-signals, and implications for the hypotheses. Completion is recommended for founder review. The scan does not answer willingness to pay or substitute sufficiency; these are appropriately reserved for customer research and Stage 1C.

**Recommended next stage:** Stage 1C — Alternatives analysis, after founder approval. No work on Stage 1C has begun.
