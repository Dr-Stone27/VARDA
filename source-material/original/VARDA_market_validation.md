## The Selection Metric

Before comparing anything, a filter — so the analysis isn't diluted by products that only *sound* similar. A product counts as a true comparable only if it passes **3 of these 4 criteria**:

| # | Criterion | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | Self-serve digital product, one-time purchase — not a subscription, not requiring a live call to receive value | SaaS tools, retainer services |
| C2 | Produces **one finished, business-specific AI-configuration artifact** — a document meant to be pasted into a system prompt or custom instructions field — built through a guided process | Generic prompt libraries (lists of prompts you run yourself), visual brand-identity templates for designers |
| C3 | Targets a business or brand context, not general personal productivity | "10x your productivity" personal prompt packs |
| C4 | Sold on Gumroad or a directly comparable indie/creator marketplace | Enterprise SaaS, agency retainers |

C2 is the decisive filter. It's the one that separates "a pile of prompts to use" from "a single document that configures the AI once." Almost everything found sounds adjacent on the surface. Very little actually clears this bar.

That's the entire list of genuine comparables. Everything else found falls into one of three adjacent-but-excluded buckets, worth naming because each one tells you something different about the market:

**Bucket 1 — Generic AI prompt packs.** By far the largest category. Products like a 2,500-prompt ChatGPT collection, a 104-prompt small business pack, a 2,100-prompt "business accelerator," and similar Notion/spreadsheet-delivered prompt libraries. These fail C2 — they hand you a library to work through yourself, not one artifact built through a guided process. Consistent pattern across every one of these: **priced $7–$40**, almost always anchored against an inflated "original" or "value" price ($200 value for $27, $3,900 down to $39). This is the pricing gravity of "AI + prompts + business" as a keyword combination on Gumroad — and it's a gravity the Passport is designed to sit above, not next to, because it isn't the same kind of product.

**Bucket 2 — Visual brand guideline templates.** InDesign/Figma/Canva templates for logo usage, color palettes, and typography — aimed at designers and agencies delivering brand identity work to clients. Fails C2 entirely (nothing to do with AI configuration) but worth citing for one reason: one listing explicitly notes real client-facing brand guideline work has commanded $10,000+, with the self-serve template version priced far below that. That's a useful structural precedent — the same "professional deliverable, templatized down" compression the Passport is doing, just in a different discipline. No consistent price was extractable for the templates themselves from what was surfaced.

**Bucket 3 — Free educational content teaching the same underlying skill.** This is the one worth taking most seriously. Multiple free articles exist that walk a reader through writing their own business system prompt — one specifically structured around the same idea (name the business, state the facts, set the rules, define the tone). This is the real substitute for a self-serve buyer with time and patience: **why pay $100 when a free guide teaches the same mechanic?** The honest answer has to be about execution, not information — which the value-gap section below addresses directly.

## The Price Peg

With only one strict comparable, a single number isn't a reliable average — but a peg is still possible by triangulating three signals rather than one:

| Signal | Value | What it tells us |
|---|---|---|
| The one true comparable (Gemma Walton) | ~$9 | The floor — for a lighter, prompt-based version of a similar idea |
| The prompt-pack gravity well (Bucket 1) | $7–$40 | What "AI + business" keyword-shoppers are conditioned to expect — the trap to avoid competing inside |
| Fiverr/Upwork done-for-you AI customization gigs *(from earlier research this session)* | $98–$520 | The ceiling — what people already pay a human to do this for them, live |

The self-serve Passport at **$100** doesn't sit inside Bucket 1's gravity well at all — it sits at the *floor* of the live-service range instead. That's a deliberate, defensible position, not an accident: the product is closer in substance to a compressed version of a $350+ guided service than to a $27 prompt pack, and the price should say so. But it also means the product cannot be found or justified by "AI prompt pack" shopping behavior — a buyer arriving with that mental model will bounce off $100 immediately. The positioning and the channel have to do the work the price comparison can't.

The Guided Build tier at **$350** sits squarely inside the already-validated Fiverr/Upwork range for done-for-you AI customization work. That number needed no new research — it was already confirmed earlier this session.

## The Value Gap — Honestly

**Where the Passport is genuinely ahead:** every comparable found requires the buyer to still do the hardest part — deciding what to say and how to structure it. The Passport removes that decision entirely; it's fill-in-the-blank with a worked example above every blank. Nothing found does that. This is a real, defensible gap, not a marketing claim.

**Where the gap is thinnest, and needs to be respected in the copy:** against the free guides specifically. The mechanical content — name the business, state the facts, set the rules, define the tone — is not proprietary; it's a known pattern, freely published. The Passport's edge over free is entirely in *execution*: the worked examples, the guided sequencing, the bundled Score Sheet and Pattern Guide, and the fact that it's a finished kit rather than a DIY writing exercise. The sales copy has to lean on that honestly — "we did the structuring for you" — not imply this is unique knowledge nobody else has.

**Where the risk sits:** because there's no dense cluster of direct comparables, there's no strong external proof that $100 converts for *this exact shape* of product. The Fiverr/Upwork ceiling validates the $350 tier well. The $100 tier is a genuine bet on a category gap, not a price copied from five competitors already clearing it. That's not a reason not to try it — it's the honest risk to carry into the first sales, and a reason to watch conversion closely rather than assume the number is right.

---

## Bottom Line

There is no crowded market to underprice or overprice against — there's a gap between cheap generic prompt packs and expensive live services, with free content sitting in between as the real competitor for attention. $100 is a defensible position inside that gap, grounded in the Fiverr/Upwork ceiling rather than the Gumroad prompt-pack floor. The thing to validate isn't "is $100 the right number" in the abstract — it's whether buyers arriving through *the channels Varda actually controls* (direct outreach, communities, eventually the taxonomy content) read this as a compressed version of real service, or default-compare it to the $27 packs sitting next to it on a Gumroad search page. That's a positioning and channel question the price alone can't answer — which is exactly why the two-phase rollout (sell it yourself first, before it sits passively on a marketplace page) matters more here than the price point does.
